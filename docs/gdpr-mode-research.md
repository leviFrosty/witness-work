# GDPR and householder data in the ministry: research for a "GDPR mode" switch

Research date: 2026-09-21. Scope: what a Jehovah's Witness publisher in the EU/EEA (and, by extension, the UK and Switzerland) may and may not record about householders under data-protection law, and what that means for WitnessWork.

This is research, not legal advice. Every substantive claim below is tied to a source. Sources are graded:

- **[P]** primary (statute, judgment, regulator, or the organisation's own published policy)
- **[S]** secondary (law-firm commentary, news reporting)
- **[L]** leaked or activist-hosted material (useful for leads; treat content as unverified)

Where a source could not be fetched, the section "What we could not verify" says so.

## 1. Plain-language primer: how GDPR works

The General Data Protection Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2016/679, "GDPR") is directly applicable law in every EU/EEA state. Full text: <https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32016R0679> [P].

### 1.1 What it covers (material scope, Art 2)

- Applies to "the processing of personal data wholly or partly by automated means and to the processing other than by automated means of personal data which form part of a filing system or are intended to form part of a filing system" (Art 2(1)). An app database is "automated means", so anything a publisher stores in WitnessWork is in scope if it is personal data.
- **Household exemption**, Art 2(2)(c): the Regulation "does not apply to the processing of personal data ... by a natural person in the course of a purely personal or household activity." Recital 18 gives examples ("correspondence and the holding of addresses, or social networking") and then adds: "However, this Regulation applies to controllers or processors which provide the means for processing personal data for such personal or household activities." The CJEU held in C-25/17 that door-to-door preaching notes are **not** a purely personal or household activity (section 2 below).

### 1.2 Key definitions (Art 4)

- **Personal data** (Art 4(1)): "any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person"; identifiable "directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier ..." A street address plus house number is normally personal data because it identifies the occupants indirectly.
- **Processing** (Art 4(2)): "any operation ... such as collection, recording, organisation, structuring, storage, ... retrieval, consultation, use, ... erasure or destruction." Writing a note is processing.
- **Filing system** (Art 4(6)): "any structured set of personal data which are accessible according to specific criteria, whether centralised, decentralised or dispersed on a functional or geographical basis."
- **Controller** (Art 4(7)): the person or body "which, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing."
- **Processor** (Art 4(8)): a body "which processes personal data on behalf of the controller."
- **Consent** (Art 4(11)): "any freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous indication of the data subject's wishes by which he or she, by a statement or by a clear affirmative action, signifies agreement."

### 1.3 Principles (Art 5)

Art 5(1) requires personal data to be: (a) processed lawfully, fairly, transparently; (b) collected for "specified, explicit and legitimate purposes" (purpose limitation); (c) "adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary" (data minimisation); (d) accurate; (e) "kept in a form which permits identification of data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes" (storage limitation); (f) kept secure. Art 5(2): the controller "shall be responsible for, and be able to demonstrate compliance with" these (accountability).

### 1.4 Lawful bases (Art 6)

Processing is lawful only if at least one Art 6(1) ground applies. The realistic ones for a publisher's notes are:

- **(a) consent** of the data subject "for one or more specific purposes";
- **(f) legitimate interests** "pursued by the controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject."

Contract (b), legal obligation (c), vital interests (d) and public task (e) do not fit a stranger met at the door.

### 1.5 Special categories (Art 9) and Recital 51

Art 9(1) prohibits processing "personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union membership, ... data concerning health or ... sex life or sexual orientation" unless an Art 9(2) exception applies. Recital 51 explains such data "merit specific protection as the context of their processing could create significant risks."

Relevant exceptions:

- **9(2)(a)**: "the data subject has given explicit consent to the processing of those personal data for one or more specified purposes."
- **9(2)(d)**: processing "in the course of its legitimate activities with appropriate safeguards by a foundation, association or any other not-for-profit body with a political, philosophical, religious or trade union aim and on condition that the processing relates solely to the members or to former members of the body or to persons who have regular contact with it in connection with its purposes and that the personal data are not disclosed outside that body without the consent of the data subjects."
- **9(2)(e)**: data "manifestly made public by the data subject."

**Does 9(2)(d) cover householders?** No. It is limited to "members or ... former members ... or ... persons who have regular contact with it in connection with its purposes." A householder met once or twice at the door is neither. The Finnish Ombudsman made exactly this point under the equivalent Directive provision: "given the absence of any membership or other pertinent relationship ... between data subjects and the applicant community ..., the collection and processing of the personal data in question always required the consent of the people in question" (ECtHR, _Jehovah's Witnesses v. Finland_, §6, quoting the Ombudsman's 2013 application) [P]. A long-standing Bible student might arguably be a person "with regular contact", but that is fact-dependent and untested; a "return visit" who has been called on twice is not.

Also note: a note that says "Catholic", "Muslim", "atheist", "not interested in religion", "used to be a Witness" or "studies with us" reveals religious or philosophical belief and is Art 9 data. The Finnish Board treated "a data subject's religious affiliation or state of health" as sensitive data when noted down (ECtHR §8) [P].

### 1.6 Transparency duties (Arts 13, 14)

When data are collected from the data subject the controller must, "at the time when personal data are obtained", tell them who the controller is, the purposes and legal basis, recipients, retention period, their rights, the right to withdraw consent, and the right to complain to a supervisory authority (Art 13(1)-(2)). If data are obtained from elsewhere (for example a neighbour, a directory, a mailbox nameplate), Art 14 applies, with a one-month deadline (Art 14(3)(a)) and a "disproportionate effort" exception (Art 14(5)(b)) that is unlikely to help a publisher who could simply speak to the person.

### 1.7 Data-subject rights (Arts 15-17)

- **Access** (Art 15(1)): confirmation of processing, a copy of the data, purposes, recipients, retention period, source.
- **Erasure** (Art 17(1)): the controller must erase "without undue delay" where (a) the data are no longer necessary, (b) consent is withdrawn and no other ground exists, (c) the subject objects, or (d) the data were unlawfully processed.

### 1.8 Joint controllers (Art 26), records (Art 30), DPIA (Art 35)

- Art 26(1): joint controllers "shall in a transparent manner determine their respective responsibilities ... by means of an arrangement between them." Art 26(3): the data subject "may exercise his or her rights ... in respect of and against each of the controllers."
- Art 30(5): the record-keeping duty does not apply to organisations under 250 staff **unless** "the processing is not occasional, or the processing includes special categories of data as referred to in Article 9(1)." Systematic ministry notes containing religion would not qualify for the exemption.
- Art 35(3)(b): a DPIA is required for "processing on a large scale of special categories of data."

### 1.9 Churches with their own rules (Art 91)

Art 91(1): where churches or religious associations "apply, at the time of entry into force of this Regulation, comprehensive rules relating to the protection of natural persons with regard to processing, such rules may continue to apply, provided that they are brought into line with this Regulation." Art 91(2) requires an independent supervisory authority. Jehovah's Witnesses in Germany operate a "Datenschutzgesetz Jehovas Zeugen" (DSGJZ) and their own supervisory authority under this Article (<https://jehovaszeugen.de/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2022/07/Datenschutzgesetz_neu-1.pdf> [P]). Its §1(9) states the DSGJZ "findet keine Anwendung auf die Verarbeitung personenbezogener Daten durch natürliche Personen zur Ausübung ausschließlich persönlicher Tätigkeiten, insbesondere persönlicher Glaubensausübung in Form des Predigtdienstes" (does not apply to processing by natural persons exercising exclusively personal activities, in particular personal exercise of faith in the form of the preaching work). Read with C-25/17, that carve-out means ministry notes are simply governed by the GDPR itself, not by the DSGJZ. Whether the DSGJZ satisfies Art 91 is disputed between German state DPAs and the organisation (artikel91.eu reporting, 2021-2025 [S]). Art 91 does not lower the standard; it only changes who supervises.

### 1.10 Enforcement, fines, liability (Arts 82, 83)

- Art 83(5): infringements of "the basic principles for processing, including conditions for consent, pursuant to Articles 5, 6, 7 and 9" and of data-subject rights (Arts 12-22) carry fines up to EUR 20,000,000 or 4 % of worldwide turnover. Art 83(4): Arts 25-39 obligations up to EUR 10,000,000 / 2 %.
- Art 82(1)-(2): any person who suffers "material or non-material damage" may claim compensation; "Any controller involved in processing shall be liable"; a processor is liable only for its own processor obligations or acting outside instructions.
- In practice, in the Finnish case no fine was imposed at all (ECtHR §97), and the obligation was a six-month compliance order (CJEU §11).

### 1.11 Territorial reach (Art 3) and who is liable for WitnessWork

- Art 3(1): applies to processing "in the context of the activities of an establishment of a controller or a processor in the Union." Art 3(2): applies to a controller or processor **not** in the Union where the processing relates to "(a) the offering of goods or services ... to such data subjects in the Union; or (b) the monitoring of their behaviour."
- **The publisher** (and, per C-25/17, the congregation/community) is the controller of householder data they choose to record. The publisher decides what to type and why.
- **The app vendor**, for data stored only on the device: Recital 18's last sentence says the Regulation "applies to controllers or processors which provide the means for processing personal data for such personal or household activities." That sentence addresses vendors that themselves process the data (cloud services, social networks). A vendor that ships software and never receives, stores or can access the content is not "processing" householder data (Art 4(2) requires an operation performed on the data) and is neither controller nor processor for it. The EDPB's Guidelines 07/2020 on controller and processor (<https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/documents/2023-10/EDPB_guidelines_202007_controllerprocessor_final_en.pdf> [P], §27) distinguish a service that merely transmits a message from the person "from whom the message originates": the provider "will not be considered as the controller in respect of the personal data contained in the message itself." That is the closest analogue for pure client-side software.
- **If data syncs to iCloud / CloudKit**: Apple, not the vendor, operates the storage, and the sync is in the user's own iCloud account. EDPB 07/2020 §30 says a cloud provider that "does not process the personal data for its own purposes and stores the data solely on behalf of its customers" is a processor. Apple's developer guidance says CloudKit data "belongs to the user" and that developers must give users a way to export and delete data in their containers (<https://developer.apple.com/icloud/allowing-users-to-manage-data/> [P]). The cautious reading: the _user_ is the controller, Apple is the user's processor, and the vendor remains outside the chain as long as the vendor's servers never receive householder data. If the vendor ever runs its own backend for householder records (friend sharing, server-side backup, analytics containing note content), the vendor becomes at least a processor and must sign Art 28 terms with each user, which is impractical for consumers. Keeping householder data device-local and user-iCloud-only is the structural choice that keeps the vendor out of controller/processor status. Vendor terms should say so explicitly.
- Note that "offering ... services ... to data subjects in the Union" (Art 3(2)(a)) is about the app's _users_, whose account/analytics data the vendor does control. That is the vendor's normal privacy-policy obligation and is separate from householder data.

### 1.12 Charter of Fundamental Rights (brief)

Full text: <https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:12012P/TXT> [P].

- **Art 7**: "Everyone has the right to respect for his or her private and family life, home and communications."
- **Art 8(1)-(2)**: "Everyone has the right to the protection of personal data concerning him or her. Such data must be processed fairly for specified purposes and on the basis of the consent of the person concerned or some other legitimate basis laid down by law. Everyone has the right of access to data which has been collected concerning him or her, and the right to have it rectified."
- **Art 10(1)**: freedom of religion, including "freedom, either alone or in community with others and in public or in private, to manifest religion or belief, in worship, teaching, practice and observance."

The CJEU read Art 10 as protecting door-to-door preaching itself (C-25/17 §§46-49) but held that this protection "does not confer an exclusively personal or household character on that activity" (§49). The ECtHR later held that requiring consent for the notes "does not ... hinder the essence of the applicant community's freedom of religion" (§95).

## 2. What C-25/17 actually decided

**Case**: _Tietosuojavaltuutettu v Jehovan todistajat — uskonnollinen yhdyskunta_, C-25/17, Grand Chamber, 10 July 2018, ECLI:EU:C:2018:551. Text read at <https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:62017CJ0025> [P]. (Curia's own viewer at <https://curia.europa.eu/juris/document/document.jsf?docid=203822&doclang=EN> renders via JavaScript and could not be scraped; EUR-Lex carries the identical official text.)

The case was decided under Directive 95/46/EC, but the relevant provisions (household exemption, filing system, controller) were carried over into the GDPR with materially the same wording (GDPR Art 2(2)(c), Art 4(6), Art 4(7)). The ECtHR in 2023 and every DPA commentary found treat the ruling as applying under the GDPR.

### 2.1 The facts the Court relied on

- §11: On 17 September 2013 the Finnish Data Protection Board adopted "a decision prohibiting the Jehovah's Witnesses Community from collecting or processing personal data in the course of door-to-door preaching carried out by its members **unless the legal requirements for processing such data** laid down, in particular, in Paragraphs 8 and 12 of the Law No 523/1999 **were satisfied**." A six-month deadline was set.
- §15: "the members of the Jehovah's Witnesses Community take notes in the course of their door-to-door preaching about visits to persons who are unknown to themselves or that Community. The data collected may consist, among other things, of the name and addresses of persons contacted, together with information concerning their religious beliefs and their family circumstances. Those data are collected as a memory aid and in order to be retrieved for any subsequent visit without the knowledge or consent of the persons concerned."
- §16: the Community "has given its members guidelines on the taking of such notes which appear in at least one of its magazines"; congregations "organise and coordinate the door-to-door preaching ... by creating maps from which areas are allocated between the members"; congregations "maintain a list of persons who have requested not to receive visits from preachers ... called the 'refusal register'"; forms for collecting data had been made available but "the use of those forms was abandoned following a recommendation by the Data Protection Supervisor."
- §19 (referring court's view): "the data collected are more than informal notes in an address book, as the notes taken concern unknown persons and contain sensitive data relating to their religious beliefs."

### 2.2 Household exemption does not apply (§§40-51)

- §40: the exemption covers "only data processing carried out in relation to an activity that is 'purely' personal or household in nature."
- §41: "The words 'personal or household' ... refer to the activity of the person processing the personal data and not to the person whose data are processed."
- §42: an activity "cannot be regarded as being purely personal or domestic where its purpose is to make the data collected accessible to an unrestricted number of people or where that activity extends, even partially, to a public space and is accordingly directed outwards from the private setting."
- §44: door-to-door preaching "is, by its very nature, intended to spread the faith ... among people who ... do not belong to the faith of the members who engage in preaching. Therefore, that activity is directed outwards from the private setting of the members who engage in preaching."
- §45: "some of the data collected ... are sent by them to the congregations ... which compile lists from that data of persons who no longer wish to receive visits ... Thus, ... those members make at least some of the data collected accessible to a potentially unlimited number of persons."
- §49: Charter Art 10 protection "does not confer an exclusively personal or household character on that activity."
- **Ruling 1**: the collection and processing "does not constitute ... the processing of personal data carried out by a natural person in the course of a purely personal or household activity."

### 2.3 Handwritten notes are a "filing system" (§§52-62)

- §57: "the requirement that the set of personal data must be 'structured according to specific criteria' is simply intended to enable personal data to be easily retrieved."
- §59: the data "are collected as a memory aid, on the basis of an allocation by geographical sector, in order to facilitate the organisation of subsequent visits ... They include not only information relating to the content of conversations concerning the beliefs of the person contacted, but also his name and address."
- §60: the criteria "among which are the name and address of persons contacted, their beliefs or their wish not to receive further visits, are chosen so that they enable data relating to specific persons to be easily retrieved."
- **Ruling 2**: a "filing system" "covers a set of personal data collected in the course of door-to-door preaching, consisting of the names and addresses and other information concerning the persons contacted, if those data are structured according to specific criteria which, in practice, enable them to be easily retrieved for subsequent use. In order for such a set of data to fall within that concept, it is not necessary that they include data sheets, specific lists or other search methods."

For an app this question does not even arise: app storage is "automated means" under Art 2(1).

### 2.4 The community is a joint controller with each publisher (§§63-75)

- §64: "In so far as only the responsibility of that community is challenged, the responsibility of the members who engage in preaching does not appear to be called into question." (The individual publisher's controller status was not disputed by anyone.)
- §67: nothing "supports a finding that the determination of the purpose and means of processing must be carried out by the use of written guidelines or instructions."
- §68: "a natural or legal person who exerts influence over the processing of personal data, for his own purposes, and who participates, as a result, in the determination of the purposes and means of that processing, may be regarded as a controller."
- §69: joint responsibility "does not require each of them to have access to the personal data concerned."
- §70: members "determine in which specific circumstances they collect personal data ..., which specific data are collected and how those data are subsequently processed", but the preaching "is ... organised, coordinated and encouraged by that community."
- §72: "the Jehovah's Witnesses Community encourages its members who engage in preaching to carry out data processing in the context of their preaching activity."
- §74: organisational autonomy of religious communities (Art 17 TFEU) does not change this: "The obligation for every person to comply with the rules of EU law on the protection of personal data cannot be regarded as an interference in the organisational autonomy of those communities."
- **Ruling 3**: a religious community "is a controller, jointly with its members who engage in preaching, ... without it being necessary that the community has access to those data, or to establish that that community has given its members written guidelines or instructions."

### 2.5 What the Court did **not** decide

The Court answered only scope/definition questions. It did not rule that note-taking is unlawful, did not specify a lawful basis, and did not address retention. Those points were dealt with by the Finnish authorities (section 3).

### 2.6 Advocate General Mengozzi's Opinion (1 February 2018), ECLI:EU:C:2018:57

Read at <https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:62017CC0025> [P]. Useful passages:

- Point 14 (facts as put by the referring court): "Lists or data sheets are the media used for collecting the data. The information in question is the name and address and a summary of the content of the conversation, relating, in particular, to religious beliefs and family circumstances."
- Point 17 (community's own position): notes "are purely personal ... The data collection relates only to data available from public sources such as the telephone directory and that data is destroyed as soon as it no longer proves useful." (The KHO and ECtHR later held that public availability is immaterial: ECtHR §94.)
- Point 57: "One of the very first criteria lending structure to that data set is the geographical criterion. To a certain extent, the member himself becomes a criterion structuring the data set in so far as the religious community allocates areas geographically."
- Point 68: "excessive formalism would make it easy to circumvent the provisions of Directive 95/46 and ... it is necessary to rely upon a more factual than formal analysis." (Quoted with approval by EDPB 07/2020, fn 6.)

## 3. The Finnish decisions: 2013 Board, KHO:2018:171, and the ECtHR in 2023

### 3.1 Sources

- The KHO's own page for KHO:2018:171 (<https://www.kho.fi/fi/index/paatokset/vuosikirjapaatokset/1544713421030.html>) now returns 404, and the Finlex page (<https://finlex.fi/fi/oikeuskaytanto/korkein-hallinto-oikeus/ennakkopaatokset/2018/171>) is JavaScript-rendered and could not be scraped. The Finnish text was therefore **not read directly**.
- The decisions are described at length, in English, in _Jehovah's Witnesses v. Finland_, ECtHR (Second Section), no. 31172/19, 9 May 2023, ECLI:CE:ECHR:2023:0509JUD003117219. Judgment PDF read from the HUDOC-identical copy at <https://johan-callewaert.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/CASE-OF-JEHOVAHS-WITNESSES-v.-FINLAND.pdf> [P]; HUDOC entry <https://hudoc.echr.coe.int/eng?i=001-224677>. Paragraph numbers below are from that judgment.
- The task brief gave the number KHO:2018:169; the correct citation is **KHO:2018:171**, 17 December 2018 (Finlex listing; ECtHR §24; Yle report 17 Dec 2018 [S] <https://yle.fi/uutiset/osasto/news/finnish_court_orders_jehovahs_witnesses_to_end_illegal_data_gathering/10558162>).

### 3.2 The 2013 Board decision prohibited collection _without consent_, not collection

- §5: already in October 2000 the Data Protection Ombudsman issued an opinion "indicating ... that personal data could be collected in the course of door-to-door preaching by individual Jehovah's Witnesses only with the consent of data subjects."
- §5: the community "confirmed that local congregations ... maintained a 'manual filing system' containing the names and addresses of people who wished not to be visited by Jehovah's Witnesses, and argued that those data subjects voluntarily and consciously 'approve[d] the collection of their data by virtue of the fact that they ask[ed] not to be visited.'"
- §6: the Ombudsman asked the Board "to prohibit the applicant community ... from collecting and otherwise processing personal data in the course of its door-to-door preaching, as well as data on foreigners and deaf people, **without the consent of those in question**," and to order the community "to give instructions to its congregations and individual Jehovah's Witnesses regarding the need to obtain consent."
- §8: on 17 September 2013 the Board "prohibited the applicant community from collecting and processing personal data in connection with door-to-door preaching without meeting the general prerequisites for processing personal and sensitive data ... that is, **without the unambiguous consent of the data subject**." The Board also said the decision "pertained to the lawfulness of processing personal data in connection with door-to-door preaching and not door-to-door preaching per se." It noted that "when, for example, a data subject's religious affiliation or state of health was noted down, sensitive data were being collected."
- §10: the Board relied on the _Our Kingdom Ministry_ leaflet "proposing what might be recorded in personal notes, namely the name and address of the person concerned, as well as information relating to his or her religious affiliation and family"; notes "were likewise sorted by territory."
- §11: "The Board ordered the applicant community to ensure, within six months, that no personal data were collected for its purposes without the prerequisites for processing such data being met. The Board did not deem it necessary to impose a fine."

### 3.3 KHO:2018:171 (17 December 2018)

- §24: the KHO "quashed the Administrative Court's decision in so far as it had annulled the Board's decision, thereby bringing the latter into force." It "stressed that the decision of the Board only concerned the applicant community and that it would not rule on whether individual Jehovah's Witnesses separately should be considered as controllers."
- §25: on the evidence of 24 written witness statements, "some of whom confirmed that they made a brief note containing personal data in connection with a return visit or Bible study **using an application on their electronic mobile device** or using a paper notebook", the notes "could include the house number, the resident's surname, if a certain address or resident at a certain address should not be visited again, and, in the case of return visits, a person's first and/or last name, gender, address ... or other contact details, the possible date of the next agreed visit and the possible topic of future discussion. Therefore, a significant part of the notes ... could indisputably be considered personal data ... and could include sensitive data."
- §26: "The fact that some individual Jehovah's Witnesses might create friendships ... or that part of the personal data collected could be available from public sources, was immaterial." "It would not make sense to take notes if, at the same time, the data were not being organised in such a manner to allow them to be found easily. Therefore, notes containing personal data in the form of paper documents or in electronic form on a mobile device ... formed a personal data file."
- §27: territory maps/cards "contained personal data about people who did not wish Jehovah's Witnesses to visit them"; the community "had to be viewed as a controller ... jointly with individual Jehovah's Witnesses who took notes."
- §80 (ECtHR summarising): the KHO's ruling did not mean "that 'personal data [could] not be processed in any circumstances in the context of door-to-door preaching'". Consent was the route.
- §93: the KHO "established that individual Jehovah's Witnesses, at least in general, did not ask data subjects to consent expressly to the processing of personal data, nor did the applicant community instruct them to do so."

### 3.4 ECtHR, 9 May 2023: consent requirement is compatible with religious freedom

- §81: applying the consent requirement "constituted an interference with the applicant community's rights under Article 9."
- §94: "data subjects had a reasonable expectation of privacy with regard to personal and sensitive data being collected and processed in the course of door-to-door preaching. The fact that some personal data might be already in the public domain neither reduces this expectation nor does it mean that such data need less protection."
- §95: "The requirement of consent by the data subject is to be considered an appropriate and necessary safeguard ... the Court cannot discern how simply asking for, and receiving, the data subject's consent would hinder the essence of the applicant community's freedom of religion. The applicant community failed to present any supporting evidence of the alleged 'chilling effect'."
- §96: the Act "applied without distinction to all religious communities and religious activities." §97: "no fine ... was imposed."
- §99: no violation of Art 9.

**Bottom line of the Finnish line of cases**: recording householder personal data in the ministry is lawful with the householder's (unambiguous, and for sensitive data explicit) consent; it is unlawful without it; asking is not an infringement of the publisher's religious freedom.

## 4. Post-2018 guidance and practice

### 4.1 The organisation's own published policies (jw.org) [P]

- Global Data Protection Policy (<https://www.jw.org/en/privacy-policy/global-data-protection-policy/>) and Global Policy on Use of Personal Data (<https://www.jw.org/en/privacy-policy/global-policy-personal-data/>) describe processing of _publishers'_ and _website users'_ data. Neither mentions householders or persons contacted in the ministry.
- Country pages for the UK, Netherlands, Germany, Denmark, Ireland (e.g. <https://www.jw.org/en/privacy-policy/use-of-personal-data/use-of-personal-data-united-kingdom/>, <https://www.jw.org/en/privacy-policy/use-of-personal-data/use-personal-data-netherlands/>, <https://www.jw.org/en/privacy-policy/use-of-personal-data/use-of-personal-data-germany/>) name the branch and local congregation as controllers _for publishers_, list legitimate interest as the main basis, give retention periods for publisher records, and give DPO addresses (`DataProtectionOfficer.GB@jw.org`, `.NL@`, `.DE@`). Again, householders are not addressed.
- Conclusion: there is no public jw.org statement on householder data. The organisation's public position is only visible through the leaked 2019 letter and press quotes.

### 4.2 The 13 May 2019 letter to congregations [L]

Reproduced on avoidjw.org (<https://avoidjw.org/news/general-data-protection-regulation/>); the PDF scans linked from that page now 404; the letter was also published by Truth & Transparency / FaithLeaks on 16 May 2019 (<https://www.truthandtransparency.org/news/2019/05/16/jehovahs-witnesses-overhaul-proselytizing-procedures-in-response-to-eu-regulation/> [S]) but T&T removed all Watch Tower documents under a July 2020 settlement (<https://www.truthandtransparency.org/press/2020/07/20/truth-transparency-settles-with-watch-tower/> [S]). The text below is the activist-site transcription and **could not be verified against an original**. It is consistent across the two independent 2019 reports and with the Dutch branch's 2018 press statements, so it is a credible lead but not a primary source.

Transcribed body (addressed to congregations in Britain and Ireland; versions in Spanish and Irish were also posted):

> "Over the years, you may have become accustomed to collecting personal data in connection with your ministry. However, recent court decisions and the enactment of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) affect the collection and use of personal data in our branch territory. Thus, any suggestions we previously made on note-taking as related to the field ministry are no longer applicable.
>
> Accordingly, publishers should not collect personal data in connection with the field ministry. This means not maintaining records that include a person's name, address, language, ethnicity, and so forth. It also includes not recording addresses where no one is at home.
>
> Though no personal data is recorded, publishers are encouraged to cultivate the interest they find. A publisher could do so by (1) providing his contact information to the interested person and arranging a return visit, (2) directing the interested person to the address of the nearest congregation meetings, or (3) directing the person to jw.org.
>
> As an exception, if a householder requests that no further visits be made to his home, the publisher should make a note of the date and address only and place it in the territory record so that publishers working the territory in the future will not call at that address."

Postscript to elders (same transcription): "Territory Map Cards (S-12) ... should show only geographic boundaries, such as street names ... These cards should not include personal data such as a list of addresses of householders who speak a particular language."

Notable: the letter chose **no collection at all** rather than a consent regime; the only permitted record is a do-not-call entry of "date and address only"; not-at-home addresses are expressly excluded. It says nothing about what happens once a person _asks_ to be contacted (gives their own number), which is where consent would come in. No S-8 form change was found in any source.

### 4.4 UK (post-Brexit) and Switzerland

- **UK GDPR** retains the same provisions with the same numbering: Art 2(2)(a) household exemption ("by an individual in the course of a purely personal or household activity") and Art 9(2)(d) not-for-profit exception limited to "members ... former members ... or persons who have regular contact" (<https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/2>, <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/9> [P]). The ICO says the domestic-purposes exclusion covers "writing to friends and family or taking pictures for your own enjoyment" and "is outside the UK GDPR's scope" only where there is "no connection to a professional or commercial activity" (<https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/exemptions/a-guide-to-the-data-protection-exemptions/> [P]). The 2019 letter was addressed to Britain and Ireland, so the organisation itself treated the UK as in scope. C-25/17 is retained EU case law for UK courts. The ICO has published no canvassing guidance for religious groups; its political-campaigning guidance (doorstep data collection needs privacy information and training) is the nearest analogue (<https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guidance-for-the-use-of-personal-data-in-political-campaigning-1/> [P]).
- **Switzerland**, Federal Act on Data Protection (FADP/nDSG, in force 1 Sept 2023), English PDF at <https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/filestore/fedlex.data.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/20230901/en/pdf-a/fedlex-data-admin-ch-eli-cc-2022-491-20230901-en-pdf-a.pdf> [P]: Art 2(2)(a) excludes data "processed by a natural person exclusively for personal use"; Art 5(c)(1) makes "data relating to religious, philosophical, political or trade union-related views or activities" sensitive, as is "affiliation to a race or ethnicity" (5(c)(2)); Art 6(3)-(4) purpose limitation and "destroyed or anonymised as soon as they are no longer required"; Art 6(6)-(7): consent "is only valid if given voluntarily for one or more specific instances of processing based on appropriate information" and "must be explicitly given for ... processing sensitive personal data"; Art 19 duty to inform at collection; Art 25 right of access (30 days); Art 30-31: a breach of personality is unlawful "unless it is justified by the consent of the data subject, by an overriding private or public interest, or by the law"; Art 32 rights to correction, deletion, prohibition. The Swiss model is justification-based rather than lawful-basis-based, but for religious data it converges on explicit consent. No FDPIC (EDÖB) statement on canvassing was found.

## 6. What consent from a householder must look like

Art 4(11) and Art 7 GDPR; EDPB Guidelines 05/2020 on consent, v1.1, 4 May 2020 (<https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/documents/files/file1/edpb_guidelines_202005_consent_en.pdf> [P]).

- **Freely given** (EDPB §13): "real choice and control"; no detriment for refusing. A householder can always refuse; no imbalance of power. This element is easy.
- **Specific** (EDPB §55): tied to "one or more specific purposes"; granular. Purpose: "so that I can come back and continue our conversation / bring the publication I promised."
- **Informed** (EDPB §64): at minimum "the controller's identity, the purpose of each of the processing operations, what (type of) data will be collected and used, the existence of the right to withdraw consent." EDPB §65: where consent is relied on by joint controllers, "these organisations should all be named." Since C-25/17 makes the congregation/community a joint controller, an honest notice names the publisher and the congregation.
- **Unambiguous / clear affirmative act** (EDPB §§75-79): "an active motion or declaration"; "Consent can be collected through a written or (a recorded) oral statement, including by electronic means." "Silence or inactivity ... cannot be regarded as an active indication of choice." Handing over a phone number and saying "call me" is a clear affirmative act for storing the number for that purpose.
- **Explicit** (Art 9(2)(a); EDPB §93): "the data subject must give an express statement of consent. An obvious way ... would be to expressly confirm consent in a written statement"; oral explicit consent recorded is acceptable (EDPB §109, hospital phone example). For religious-belief notes, the publisher would need something like "Is it OK if I note that you're Catholic so I remember what we discussed?" and a yes.
- **Withdrawable** (Art 7(3); EDPB §§113-117): "as easy to withdraw as to give"; the subject must be told of this before consenting; after withdrawal, data with no other basis "should be deleted."
- **Demonstrable** (Art 7(1); EDPB §§104-108): "The burden of proof will be on the controller." "The controller may keep a record of consent statements received, so he can show how consent was obtained, when consent was obtained and the information provided to the data subject at the time." §106: proof should not itself create "excessive amounts of additional data processing." §110-111: no fixed lifetime for consent, but "consent should be refreshed at appropriate intervals."
- **Timing** (EDPB §90): "consent must always be obtained before the controller starts processing."

**Can an app capture this?** Yes, in a limited but useful way. A per-contact "consent obtained" toggle with a timestamp, plus a fixed on-screen script of what the householder was told (identity, purpose, data kept, right to withdraw, how to withdraw), gives the publisher the Art 7(1) record EDPB §108 describes: how, when, and what information. The app cannot prove the words were actually spoken; nothing can, short of a signature or recording, and the ECtHR did not require more than "simply asking for, and receiving, the data subject's consent" (§95). What the app _should not_ do is let a contact with name/phone/notes exist in an EU-mode record with the consent toggle off.

## 7. Retention and erasure

- Art 5(1)(e): identifiable data kept "for no longer than is necessary for the purposes." Art 17(1)(a): erase when "no longer necessary." Swiss FADP Art 6(4): "destroyed or anonymised as soon as they are no longer required."
- Neither the GDPR nor any decision found sets a number of days for ministry notes. The purpose is "to prepare for subsequent visits" (CJEU §60). Once no return visit is planned, the purpose is spent.
- The Dutch branch said in 2018 that records are removed "when someone stops" (RD [S]); the community told the CJEU that data "is destroyed as soon as it no longer proves useful" (AG Opinion point 17); the unverified Danish position accepted destruction "after completing the district review".
- **Is an auto-delete after N days defensible?** Yes, and it is the standard way to operationalise storage limitation; Art 13(2)(a) requires telling the subject "the period for which the personal data will be stored, or ... the criteria used." A rule such as "contact records with no visit logged for 90 days are deleted; do-not-call entries are kept indefinitely; not-at-home markers are cleared when the territory is returned or after 14 days" is a defensible articulation of "criteria used". A shorter window is safer; the risk in a longer window is Art 5(1)(e), and the risk in a very short one is only user annoyance. EDPB §111 recommends refreshing consent at intervals, which a "still in touch? keep / delete" prompt at expiry satisfies.
- Do-not-call entries are the exception: their purpose (never visiting again) has no natural end, so indefinite retention of _date + address only_ is consistent with Art 5(1)(e). A periodic review is good practice but not required by any source found.

## 8. Do-not-call lists

- Facts: congregations kept a "refusal register" / "prohibition register" of "persons who have requested not to receive visits" (CJEU §16; ECtHR §17). The community argued householders "approve[d] the collection of their data by virtue of the fact that they ask[ed] not to be visited" and "the congregations could not comply with such a request without collecting the person's name and address" (ECtHR §5).
- The CJEU used those lists as evidence that data left the private sphere (§45) and as a structuring criterion (§60); it did not say they were unlawful.
- The 2019 letter retains them but minimises them to "the date and address only." That is the correct data-minimisation answer: an address alone lets future publishers skip the door; a name adds nothing to that purpose.
- Lawful basis: Art 6(1)(f), legitimate interest. The interest is the householder's own (Art 6(1)(f) allows "legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party"), and the balancing test is one-sided because the processing exists to honour the householder's wish. The ICO's direct-marketing guidance treats suppression lists the same way: keeping a "do not contact" list is how you respect an objection (<https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guidance-on-direct-marketing-using-electronic-mail/what-else-do-we-need-to-consider/> [P], marketing context, by analogy).
- No DPA opinion specifically on religious do-not-call lists was found (see section 10). Confidence that a date + address entry is lawful is high; adding a name or the reason ("angry", "Catholic") is not.

## 9. Recommendations for a "GDPR mode" switch

Design goal: with the switch on, the app should make it structurally hard to create an unlawful record, and easy to honour access, erasure and withdrawal. The app does not become a controller by doing this; it "provides the means" and can choose safe defaults (Art 25 data protection by design applies to controllers, but shipping privacy-by-default is how the vendor stays out of trouble and helps its users).

### 9.1 Scope and defaults

1. **Region default**: turn the mode on by default when the device region or locale is any EU/EEA member state, the UK, or Switzerland; allow manual override in both directions (a publisher on holiday abroad, or a non-EU publisher who wants the stricter behaviour). Do not tie it to App Store storefront only; use device region plus an explicit setting. Show a one-time explanation when auto-enabled.
2. **Name the mode neutrally** in UI ("Data protection mode (EU/UK/CH)") rather than "GDPR mode"; Switzerland is not GDPR.

### 9.2 Fields to hide or disable (EU mode on)

3. **Householder name, phone, email, address/GPS pin**: keep the fields, but they are unavailable until the per-contact **"Householder agreed I may keep their details"** toggle is switched on. Toggle stores a timestamp. Show the short consent script (9.5) next to it.
4. **Free-text notes**: available only behind the consent toggle, with a persistent one-line hint: "Do not note religion, health, family situation, language or ethnicity." Consider a separate **"Sensitive details (explicit consent)"** toggle that must be on before the notes field expands beyond a short "topic / scripture / publication" line. This mirrors the Art 6 vs Art 9 split.
5. **Structured religion / church / language / ethnicity / family-status fields**: hide entirely in EU mode. There is no safe default for them and the 2019 letter names them.
6. **Photos** of people or houses: disable attachment in EU mode.
7. **Not-at-home per-address markers**: either disable, or allow only as a **territory working list** that is device-local, never synced, never exported, and auto-cleared after a short fixed period (14 days is a reasonable default) or when the user marks the territory finished. Never attach a name.
8. **Bible-study flag** attached to a contact: require the explicit-consent toggle.
9. **Sharing / export of contact records to other users** (friend sharing, AirDrop, congregation lists): disable for householder records in EU mode, except the do-not-call address list, and warn that sharing makes data "accessible to a potentially unlimited number of persons" (CJEU §45).

### 9.3 Fields to keep

10. Publisher's own time, placements, videos, own return-visit and study _counts_, service reports, goals, schedule: unaffected. These are the user's data.
11. **Do-not-call**: keep, limited to address + date (+ optional "house number only" granularity). No name, no reason, no notes. Keep indefinitely; offer a review reminder yearly.
12. Territory boundaries (street names, map polygons) without any per-household data.

### 9.4 Retention

13. Default retention for consented contact records in EU mode: delete contacts with no logged interaction for **90 days**, with a prompt 7 days before ("Still in touch with this person? Keep / Delete") that resets the clock if kept. Make the window user-configurable within a bounded range (30-365 days). Show the window in the consent script so Art 13(2)(a) is satisfied.
14. On deletion, hard-delete on device and issue a CloudKit delete; do not keep a tombstone with the name.

### 9.5 Consent capture

15. Per-contact toggle + timestamp + version of the consent script shown. Store nothing else about how consent was obtained (EDPB §106).
16. Suggested script (shown to the publisher to say, and stored as the "information provided"):
    "May I keep your name and [phone/address] on my phone so I can come back as we agreed? Only I [and my congregation] will have it, I will only use it to visit or contact you about our conversation, I delete it after [90 days] if we lose touch, and you can ask me to delete it at any time."
17. Withdrawal: a one-tap "Householder asked me to delete their details" action on the contact that deletes immediately and optionally converts the address into a do-not-call entry (date + address only) if the householder also asked not to be visited.

### 9.6 Rights affordances

18. **Export**: per-contact "Show what I have about this person" screen and a plain-text/PDF export, so a publisher can answer an Art 15 access request at the door.
19. **Erase all EU-mode householder data**: single action in settings, in addition to per-contact deletion.
20. **Search by address**: when a householder asks "do you have anything on me?", the publisher needs to find the record by address, not only by name.

### 9.7 In-app disclaimer wording (suggested)

> "Data protection mode is on. Courts in the EU have held that notes about people met in the ministry are covered by data-protection law (CJEU C-25/17; ECtHR _Jehovah's Witnesses v. Finland_, 2023). You, and your congregation, are responsible for what you record. In this mode WitnessWork only lets you store a householder's details after they have agreed, hides fields for religion, health, language, ethnicity and family situation, does not record not-at-home addresses, and deletes contacts you have not visited in [90] days. Do-not-call entries store the date and address only. WitnessWork never receives or stores your householder records; they stay on your device and, if you enable it, in your own iCloud account. This is not legal advice. Follow your branch's current direction."

### 9.8 Vendor-side

21. Privacy policy: state explicitly that householder records are processed on-device and in the user's own iCloud, that the vendor has no access, and that the user is the controller of those records.
22. Do not add any server-side feature (analytics, backup, sharing relay) that carries householder record content, or the vendor becomes a processor with Art 28 obligations toward every EU user.
23. Consider the same defaults globally later: nothing in this document is EU-specific in principle; the EU is just where a court has said it out loud.

### 11.3 Where the switch lives

- Preference: add `gdprMode` (or a neutral name such as `dataProtectionMode`) to `PREFERENCE_DEFAULTS` in `src/stores/preferences.ts:243`. List it in `NON_SYNCABLE_PREFERENCE_KEYS` (`:832-872`) so it is per-device, mirroring `iCloudSyncIncludeImages`. Bump the persist `version` and migrate.
- Onboarding: add a `StepId` and `StepDef` in `src/features/onboarding/components/Onboarding.tsx:42-138` immediately after `privacyFirst`. The `PrivacyFirst.tsx` step is the natural template.
- Region default: `getLocales()[0].regionCode` from `expo-localization` (already used in `src/lib/locales.ts:53`) against an EU/EEA + UK + CH set. `formatRegion` (`src/lib/dates.ts:29-61`) is a weaker signal because it is a formatting preference, not a country.
- This is a user preference, not a PostHog `FeatureFlag` (`src/lib/featureFlags.ts`).
- Per-contact consent needs new fields on `Contact`: `consentGivenAt?: string`, `consentScriptVersion?: number`, `explicitConsentGivenAt?: string`. They must travel through `SyncPayload`, `contactShareLink` policies (if sharing stays enabled anywhere), the MyTime import mapper, and the JSON backup.

### 11.4 Gaps that exist regardless of EU mode

- No user-facing "delete all my data" action; the only full wipe is in the dev Tools screen (`src/app/navigation/ToolsScreen.tsx:1700`). Section 9.6 item 19 needs a real one.
- MMKV is not configured with encryption at rest (`src/stores/mmkv.ts:6`). Device encryption covers this on iOS, but it is worth stating in the privacy policy.
- Privacy policy (`src/constants/links.ts:5`) should say explicitly that householder records stay on-device and in the user's own iCloud, and that the user is the controller of them (section 9.8 item 21). It should also disclose the proxy-mediated HERE, share-link and Notes Import paths above, since those _do_ touch vendor infrastructure.
